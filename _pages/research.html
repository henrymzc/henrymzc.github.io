Job Market Paper

Post-Study Work Visa, Global Graduate Education and Skilled Immigrant EmploymentJMP

Zhangchi Ma and Yangfan Wang

Immigration Education Skilled Labor
PDF Slides

We study how tightening post-study work visa policy jointly shapes international students' decisions of where to study and where to work. We leverage the 2012 UK visa reform, which abolished a two-year unconditional post-study work visa, to quantify how sensitive international master's students' education choices are to labor-market access in the destination country. Using a novel longitudinal panel of LinkedIn profiles, we first find that the policy brought large unintended hiring frictions in the high-skilled sector. Applying a difference-in-differences design, we further identify a significant decline in UK master's enrollment among Indian students. To interpret this response and separate its channels, we then develop a two-stage dynamic model of education and post-graduation work choices. The model attributes over one-third of the loss in high-skilled labor to the indirect deterrence effect on the education margin, a share that is even larger for STEM majors. We further find that a one-percent loss in skilled labor positions is associated with a 0.7\% loss of students for STEM and a 0.5\% loss for non-STEM. Our results highlight that a reform targeting low-skilled employment can create negative spillovers to high-skilled hiring, and quantify the cost of shrinking employment opportunities for education exports.

Bail Decision and Racial Descrimination

Bocar A. Ba, Xavier D'Haultfœuille, Zhangchi Ma, and Arnaud Maurel

PDF

This paper investigates the determinants of bail decisions. We focus on the determinants of bail amounts set by the judges, a dimension that has not received much attention in the literature. To do so, we specify a structural model of the optimal bail amount, in which judges set the bail amount that minimizes the welfare cost of releasing the defendant. Our model allows for multiple dimensions of defendant-level unobserved heterogeneity, some being private information to the defendant and some being observed by the judge but not by econometricians. We establish constructive identification of the model parameters, including the welfare weights assigned by the judges to different groups of defendants, and derive a simple estimation procedure. We estimate our model using data from Chicago, focusing on whether the observed bail amounts are consistent with racial bias.

Perceived Discrimination and Job Search among First- and Second-Generation Americans

Zhangchi Maspan>, Arnaud Maurel, Angelino Viceisza, and Daniel Y. Xu

PDF

This paper investigates the relationship between perceived discrimination and job search behavior among first- and second-generation Americans. We elicit beliefs regarding two distinct domains of discrimination: the ``current workplace'' and the ``job search process''. We also conduct an information intervention experiment to see if light-touch population-level information would shift idiosyncratic beliefs and job search behavior. We first document that immigrants perceive substantial disparities between themselves and a counterfactual native in both domains. In addition, immigrants overestimate the native–immigrant earnings gap in their occupation. Providing population-level earnings statistics successfully shifts posterior beliefs and reduces over-pessimism among treated participants in both domains. In a five-month follow-up, we find that higher perceived discrimination in both domains predicts increased search probabilities and efforts. Using initial population belief errors and treatment assignment as instruments, we find that a one-standard-deviation increase in perceived discrimination raises the probability of preparatory job search by 33.5 percentage points. These results suggest that immigrants respond to perceived unfairness in their current work or in the job search process by intensifying their search effort, rather than becoming discouraged. Furthermore, providing accurate market information might decrease search intensity.\\

Model Misspecification in Dynamic Discrete Choice Models with Choice Friction

Zhangchi Ma

PDF

In static discrete choice models, a friction that probabilistically removes alternatives from agents' choice sets is observationally equivalent to a degradation of those alternatives' deterministic utilities. We analyze a two-stage discrete choice model in which a downstream friction restricts access to a preferred alternative with regime-specific probability $\lambda$, and contrast it with a naive specification that assumes a full choice set and attributes the same observed restrictions to a change in underlying utility. We show that under i.i.d. log-concave error distribution, the naive specification always \textbf{understates the level} of the inclusive value in any single regime. Furthermore, its bias on the \textbf{change} in the inclusive value for different $\lambda$, and hence on the predicted upstream response, can go in either direction, over- or under-predicting depending on the parameters. Under Type-I Extreme Value errors, we derive \textbf{ closed-form expressions} for the implied upstream (Stage-1) inclusive value response. We then show that the misspecification is testable under Type-I Extreme Value errors: when the econometrician observes the upstream choice response, all structural parameters---latent utility, pre- and post-reform arrival rates, and the upstream taste parameter---are point-identified in closed form from four observed choice probabilities under a calibrated discount factor.
