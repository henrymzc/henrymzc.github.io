Job Market Paper

Visa Policy, Global Education and Labor Markets

with Yangfan Wang

Draft Coming Soon

We study how post-study work visa policy jointly shapes international students' decisions of where to study and where to work. We exploit the 2012 UK visa reform, which abolished a two-year unconditional post-study work visa with the aim of curbing low-skill employment, to quantify the effects of reduced job access on Indian students. Using a novel longitudinal panel of LinkedIn profiles, we first document sharp declines in enrollment and stay rates for UK master's students after the policy. Applying a difference-in-differences design, we causally identify that the reform reduces UK master's enrollment by an additional 4%–7.3% for each percentage point higher pre-reform UK stay rate at students' undergraduate institutions. Enrollment responses are similar whether past graduates stayed in the UK for high- or low-skill jobs. To interpret the response and separate its channels, we then develop a two-stage discrete choice model of education and post-graduation work. The model attributes most of the decline in bachelor-degree labor supply to the reform's direct effect, which also drives a 53% decline in student enrollment and thereby indirectly reduces the supply of advanced-degree Indian workers by 58%. Our results highlight how a reform targeting low-skill employment can unintentionally generate negative spillovers to high-skill sectors through both direct and indirect channels.

Bail Decision and Racial Discrimination

with Bocar A. Ba, Xavier D'Haultfœuille, and Arnaud Maurel

Draft Coming Soon

This paper investigates the determinants of bail decisions. We focus on the determinants of bail amounts set by the judges, a dimension that has not received much attention in the literature. To do so, we specify a structural model of the optimal bail amount, in which judges set the bail amount that minimizes the welfare cost of releasing the defendant. Our model allows for multiple dimensions of defendant-level unobserved heterogeneity, some being private information to the defendant and some being observed by the judge but not by econometricians. We establish constructive identification of the model parameters, including the welfare weights assigned by the judges to different groups of defendants, and derive a simple estimation procedure. We estimate our model using data from Chicago, focusing on whether the observed bail amounts are consistent with racial bias.

Perceived Discrimination and Job Search among First- and Second-Generation Americans

with Arnaud Maurel, Angelino Viceisza, and Daniel Y. Xu

Draft Coming Soon

This paper investigates the relationship between perceived discrimination and job search behavior among first- and second-generation Americans. We elicit beliefs regarding two distinct domains of discrimination: the "current workplace" and the "job search process". We also conduct an information intervention experiment to see if light-touch population-level information would shift idiosyncratic beliefs and job search behavior. We first document that immigrants perceive substantial disparities between themselves and a counterfactual native in both domains. In addition, immigrants overestimate the native–immigrant earnings gap in their occupation. Providing population-level earnings statistics successfully shifts posterior beliefs and reduces over-pessimism among treated participants in both domains. In a five-month follow-up, we find that higher perceived discrimination in both domains predicts increased search probabilities and efforts. Using initial population belief errors and treatment assignment as instruments, we find that a one-standard-deviation increase in perceived discrimination raises the probability of preparatory job search by 33.5 percentage points. These results suggest that immigrants respond to perceived unfairness in their current work or in the job search process by intensifying their search effort, rather than becoming discouraged. Furthermore, providing accurate market information might decrease search intensity.

College Accessibility, Quota Relocation and Job Location Choice

with Yihong Liu

Manuscript

Access to higher education significantly influences social equality and intergenerational mobility, which is directly governed by the regional college admission quota system in China. The Chinese government initiated the "Cooperative Plan of College Enrollment in the Mid-western Province" (CPCE) in 2008, mandating some colleges in provinces with rich education resources to allocate admission quotas to Mid-western provinces. Using the Difference-in-Difference strategy, we find that while the policy led to lower entrance test scores for students from recipient provinces, these individuals did not exhibit inferior academic performance compared to their counterparts enrolled before the policy. Moreover, the policy induced students with stronger desire to work in metropolitan labor markets post-graduation to enroll in colleges located in eastern provinces, resulting in more Mid-western students working in China's top-four cities.

Disaster under control: droughts, locust outbreaks, and state capacity in premodern China, 1644-1911

with Yu Hao

Undergraduate Thesis, 2019

Manuscript

Locust outbreaks, which are normally caused by drought, pose a major threat to agrarian societies. Nonetheless, state intervention could effectively mitigate the impacts of droughts on locusts by coordinating and enforcing antilocust actions. Based on a provincial panel dataset for Qing China (1644-1911), we find locust outbreaks to be positively related to droughts in current and previous years. A within-province centralization reform imposed in the 1720s mitigated the impacts of drought on locust outbreaks in current and previous years. A further investigation shows that the mitigation effect is nonmonotonic with the degree of centralization, suggesting that both central coordination and local information were important. In addition, the reform enhanced bureaucratic control over local officials in times of crisis, leading to more effective locust control.

Model Misspecification in Dynamic Discrete Choice Models with Choice Friction

In static discrete choice models, a friction that probabilistically removes alternatives from agents' choice sets is observationally equivalent to a degradation of those alternatives' deterministic utilities. We analyze a two-stage discrete choice model in which a downstream friction restricts access to a preferred alternative with regime-specific probability $\lambda$, and contrast it with a naive specification that assumes a full choice set and attributes the same observed restrictions to a change in underlying utility. We show that under i.i.d. log-concave error distribution, the naive specification always understates the level of the inclusive value in any single regime. Furthermore, its bias on the change in the inclusive value for different $\lambda$, and hence on the predicted upstream response, can go in either direction, over- or under-predicting depending on the parameters. Under Type-I Extreme Value errors, we derive closed-form expressions for the implied upstream (Stage-1) inclusive value response. We then show that the misspecification is testable under Type-I Extreme Value errors: when the econometrician observes the upstream choice response, all structural parameters—latent utility, pre- and post-reform arrival rates, and the upstream taste parameter—are point-identified in closed form from four observed choice probabilities under a calibrated discount factor.
